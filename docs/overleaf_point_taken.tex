\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This study tests whether giving people short AI-generated coaching tips between
rounds of a debate game helps them make better arguments over time. Participants
play three rounds against a fair AI opponent, and we compare how much their
argument quality improves across rounds when they get coaching versus when they
don't. The design builds on Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245), who showed that
practicing with an AI partner and getting feedback improves empathic
communication; we ask the same kind of question about arguing.
Each round is scored on six dimensions by an AI judge, following the evaluation
approach of Kumar et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025). This document
lays out the design, the measures, the prompts, and the analyses, and is frozen
before the full data collection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online, disagreement breaks down fast. Part of the reason is the medium itself:
when people cannot hear tone or see body language, they misread each other. There
is evidence for this. When people hear an opinion they disagree with instead of
reading it, they judge the other person as more thoughtful and are less likely to
dehumanize them (Schroeder, Kardas, \& Epley, 2017), which suggests that text
strips out the cues that keep disagreement constructive.

Structured argument games are one way to push back on this. Point Taken has people
build, place, and revise arguments in a more deliberate way than a normal comment
thread, and an earlier pilot ($N = 38$) found that, compared to unstructured
discussion, it increased understanding of opposing positions, intellectual
humility, and openness to learning (Elnakouri et al., 2025).

A separate line of work shows that practicing with an AI partner and getting
personalized feedback can improve how people communicate: Kumar et al.
(arXiv:2603.15245) demonstrated this for empathic communication. What has not been
tested is whether the same practice-plus-coaching approach helps people build
better arguments. That is a different skill, more about logic and evidence than
emotion, and it is the gap this study addresses.

\paragraph{Research question.} Does short, AI-generated coaching between rounds of
an argument game improve the quality of people's arguments over repeated practice,
more than practice alone? I expect it will. Specifically, I think the coaching will
make fallacies visible and help people structure their arguments more clearly.

The rest of this document covers why it is worth doing now
(Section~\ref{sec:whynow}), the design, procedure, and measures
(Sections~\ref{sec:design}--\ref{sec:measures}), how the scoring works and how I
validate it (Section~\ref{sec:judge}), and the preregistered analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). The full prompts are in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts}.

\section{Why now}
\label{sec:whynow}

Two things make this the right time to study it. First, AI is increasingly part of
how people argue and reason online, and learning to argue well with and around it
is becoming its own skill worth supporting. Second, the study only became runnable
recently: it needs an AI that can argue back in good faith and adapt to a person in
real time, and an AI that can score argument quality reliably enough to serve as an
outcome measure. That second piece was only recently established (Kumar et al.,
\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025). A couple of years ago, neither piece
worked well enough to run this experiment.

\section{Study design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiment uses a between-subjects design with two conditions, to which
participants are randomly assigned (50/50) on first entry:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Control:} three rounds of the argument game with no coaching.
  \item \textbf{Treatment (AICoach):} the same three rounds, with a between-round
  coaching interface.
\end{itemize}

Everything else is identical across the two groups: the topics, the AI opponent,
the three rounds, and the scoring. The only difference is whether the
between-round coaching message appears. In each round a participant argues against the AI opponent, which lets us run one
participant at a time online without having to match people in real time. Each round uses one
topic, and the topic order is randomized per participant so that topic is not tied
to round.

Each person stays in one group the whole time so the coaching can't leak into the
no-coach comparison. If someone got coached in one round, that would likely change
how they argue afterward even without coaching, which would contaminate what "no
coaching" looks like. Keeping people in one group keeps the control condition
clean.

The AI opponent argues in good faith. We want to measure how well someone builds
an argument against a fair partner. If the opponent used cheap shots or
straw-manned them, a participant's response would partly reflect how well they
resist bad tactics, which is a different question.

\section{Procedure: rounds and interaction requirements}
\label{sec:procedure}

The game is three rounds, one topic per round (topic order randomized), and takes
roughly 15--20 minutes total. To finish a round, a participant has to make at
least three argument turns, and at least one of those turns has to directly
answer something the opponent actually said. That reply requirement is what makes the \emph{engagement} dimension
measurable, since it forces the two sides to actually interact. The exact minimum
thresholds are noted as an open parameter in Section~\ref{sec:open}.

\section{Measures}
\label{sec:measures}

Each round is scored by an AI judge on \textbf{six dimensions}, each rated 1--5.
Following the structure of Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245), the six split into
three "helpful" behaviors and three "counterproductive" ones:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Helpful (shown to treatment participants):} reasoning quality,
  engagement, constructive stance.
  \item \textbf{Counterproductive (analysis only, not shown):} fallacies,
  evasion, hostility.
\end{itemize}

Two round-level measures are derived:
\begin{align*}
  \texttt{composite} &= \text{mean of the three helpful dimensions} \quad (1\text{--}5),\\
  \texttt{analysis\_overall} &= \textstyle\sum(\text{helpful}) - \sum(\text{counterproductive}).
\end{align*}
The \texttt{composite} is the primary dependent measure; \texttt{analysis\_overall}
is the full six-dimension score, mirroring the prescriptive-minus-proscriptive
composite of the source paradigm.

Only the three helpful dimensions are shown back to participants in the treatment
group. The counterproductive ones are kept behind the scenes for analysis, which
keeps people from optimizing a single number. The three counterproductive
dimensions also line up with the point-level fallacy categories in
Appendix~\ref{sec:pointlevel} (overgeneralization maps to fallacies, irrelevant
rebuttal to evasion, and personal attack or straw man to hostility), so the
per-argument feedback and the round-level score are tracking the same behaviors at
two levels of detail.

Participants additionally complete a baseline survey (topic stances and four
self-reported disagreement-style items) and an exit survey.

\subsection{Why these six dimensions}
\label{sec:whydims}

The six dimensions split into three that help an argument and three that hurt it.
I chose them to cover both what good arguing adds and what it should avoid, and
each one maps onto established work.

Helpful (shown to treatment participants):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Reasoning quality}: whether claims are backed by a reason,
  example, or evidence, and whether the conclusion actually follows. Good
  reasoning uses logic and evidence; weak reasoning is just opinion. This is what
  Wachsmuth et al. (2017) call \emph{cogency}: a claim should be acceptable (worth
  believing), relevant (it supports the point), and sufficient (there is enough
  behind it). The Discourse Quality Index (Steenbergen et al., 2003) captures the
  same idea as \emph{justification}.
  \item \emph{Engagement}: whether you take on the other side's strongest point
  and represent it fairly. This is the dialectical side of Wachsmuth's taxonomy
  (whether an argument holds up against the real counterarguments), and it lines up
  with the rules of critical discussion in pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren \&
  Grootendorst), which say you have to address the actual standpoint. Skipping this
  is straw-manning.
  \item \emph{Constructive stance}: whether you keep it about the idea, grant fair
  points, and stay open to changing your mind. This maps
  onto two Discourse Quality Index codes: \emph{respect} (toward the other person
  and their argument) and \emph{constructive politics} (openness to build on or
  revise a position).
\end{itemize}

Counterproductive (scored for analysis, not shown; a higher score means more of
the bad behavior):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Fallacies}: overgeneralizing, false cause, and exaggeration.
  These are failures of the same cogency criteria above, and they are classic
  informal fallacies (Walton, 1995). I focus on the four most common and
  highest-impact ones I saw in the earlier game data: vague claims, over-broad
  claims, causal leaps, and irrelevant responses.
  \item \emph{Evasion}: dodging the other point without attempting a real
  counterargument. This is a relevance failure and the flip side of engagement.
  \item \emph{Hostility}: going after the person emotionally, which is an ad
  hominem. This is the opposite of the Discourse Quality Index's \emph{respect},
  and a classic fallacy in its own right.
\end{itemize}

The three helpful dimensions capture what good arguing adds; the three
counterproductive ones capture what it should avoid. Splitting it this way (three
prescriptive, three proscriptive, with overall $=$ helpful $-$ counterproductive)
mirrors the paradigm we are replicating, and it lets the coaching push people
toward the good moves and away from the bad ones.

\section{LLM-as-judge and validation}
\label{sec:judge}

Each round is scored by one call to the AI judge (\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}); the
scoring prompt is in Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts}. Since the main result depends on
this judge, I check that it scores well, following the evaluation approach of Kumar
et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025).

To do that, a human expert (and ideally a second coder, to check they agree with
each other) will hand-score a sample of about 20--30 rounds on the same rubric. I
then compare the AI's scores to the human scores using Krippendorff's $\alpha$, and
check that against how well the two humans agreed with each other. Any dimension
where the AI doesn't match well gets its scoring instructions revised before the
real study runs. The same expert-scored rounds also seed the few-shot examples in
the judge prompt. This happens on the pilot data, before the full run.

\section{Preregistered analysis plan}
\label{sec:analysis}

I am freezing these analyses before the full data collection. The pilot ($n = 5$)
is only to check the pipeline and timing; I do not use it as data.

\paragraph{Primary hypothesis (H1).} The coached group's argument-quality score
climbs more across the three rounds than the control group's does. Statistically
this is the condition-by-round interaction in a mixed-effects model,
\[
  \texttt{composite} \sim \text{condition} \times \text{round}_c + (1 \mid \text{participant}),
\]
with round centered ($0,1,2$). A positive interaction supports H1, and I report it
with its 95\% CI. If that model doesn't fit cleanly at this sample size, I fall back
to a round-1-vs-round-3 change-score comparison (a Welch $t$-test).

\paragraph{Secondary (H2--H3).} I run the same model on the full six-dimension
score (\texttt{analysis\_overall}) and on the summed counterproductive score, to
see whether coaching also cuts down fallacies, evasion, and hostility across rounds.

\paragraph{Exploratory (H4).} I look at each dimension on its own to see which ones
move most. This is descriptive and I don't correct for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Prespecified exclusions.} A participant is dropped if they don't finish
all three rounds; if they fail an effort check (for example, a whole round of empty
or one-character non-answers); if they self-report using AI assistance (such as
ChatGPT) to write their arguments, the same exclusion used in the paper I replicate
(Kumar et al., arXiv:2603.15245); if they show up as a duplicate Prolific ID; or if
a round's score had to fall back to error text because the coach or opponent AI
failed (flagged via the exported \texttt{model\_name} field). I report how many I
drop and why.

\paragraph{Sample.} About 30 per group (~60 total). That isn't from a power
calculation; at this size I treat the study as a first estimate of the effect and
its confidence interval, to set up a properly powered follow-up.

I run all of this from \texttt{analysis/e18\_analysis.ipynb} on the data export.

\section{Model prompts (verbatim)}
\label{sec:prompts}

The following are the exact prompts used in the live study.

\subsection{Round-scoring (judge) prompt}

Each dimension carries explicit 1/3/5 anchors, and rationales must cite specific
text. The reasoning-quality anchors operationalize the acceptability, relevance,
and sufficiency criteria of the argument-quality taxonomy (Wachsmuth et al.,
2017); the constructive-stance and hostility anchors follow the respect and
openness constructs of the Discourse Quality Index (Steenbergen et al., 2003).

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are an expert annotator scoring how well a participant argued in one
round of a structured debate game. Score each of six behaviors on a 1-5
scale using the anchors below. Judge only the participant's arguments, in
the context of the topic and the opponent's points, and base every score
on specific evidence in their text.

HELPFUL behaviors (constructive argumentation):
1. reasoning_quality — are the claims acceptable, relevant to the point,
   and sufficiently supported (a reason, example, mechanism, or evidence)?
   1 = bare assertions or non-sequiturs; 3 = partial or general support;
   5 = each key claim is specific and backed by a clear reason or example.
2. engagement — does the participant take on the opponent's strongest
   point and represent it fairly, rather than talking past it?
   1 = ignores the opponent; 3 = responds to a weaker version;
   5 = directly answers the opponent's strongest point, stated fairly.
3. constructive_stance — critiques the idea (not the person), grants valid
   points, stays open?
   1 = closed or dismissive; 3 = civil but no openness;
   5 = engages the idea and concedes or builds on a fair point.

COUNTERPRODUCTIVE behaviors (score HIGHER when MORE present):
4. fallacies — overgeneralization, false cause, exaggeration.
   1 = none; 3 = one clear instance; 5 = pervasive.
5. evasion — ignoring points, shifting subject, whataboutism.
   1 = stays on point; 3 = dodges once; 5 = repeatedly avoids.
6. hostility — personal attacks, straw-manning, dismissiveness.
   1 = none; 3 = one loaded remark; 5 = repeated bad-faith attacks.
{examples}
Topic: {topic}
The participant's arguments this round:
{arguments}

Score strictly against the anchors; each rationale must quote or point to
specific text. Respond with ONLY a JSON object with all six keys plus a
"feedback" field (one or two forward-looking tips for the next round).
\end{Verbatim}

The \texttt{\{examples\}} field receives expert-coded few-shot exemplars, following
the finding of Kumar et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025) that a
detailed framework plus a small number of scored examples improves judge
reliability over zero-shot. Two illustrative examples currently seed this slot and
will be replaced with expert-coded rounds prior to the full run
(Section~\ref{sec:judge}).

\subsection{Between-round coaching prompt (primary intervention)}

The coaching shown to treatment participants between rounds uses a tutor-style
structure: identify a strength, focus on the participant's lowest-scoring helpful
dimension, and provide a concrete rewrite of one weaker argument. This mirrors the
personalized-feedback depth of the practice-plus-coaching paradigm
(arXiv:2603.15245).

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are an argument coach giving one participant feedback between rounds
of a debate game. You have the arguments they just made and their rubric
scores. Help them argue better next round, the way a good writing tutor
would: specific, warm, and focused on one or two changes.

Write feedback in three short parts, addressed to "you":
1. Strength: name one thing they did well, quoting their own words.
2. Focus: take the LOWEST-scoring helpful dimension and explain, in plain
   language, the single change that would move it most. Tie it to a
   specific argument of theirs.
3. Try this: rewrite ONE of their weaker arguments into a stronger version
   that keeps their side and intent, so they see the change concretely.

Under 120 words. Encouraging, no jargon, no numeric scores in the text.

Topic: {topic}
Their arguments: {arguments}
Helpful-dimension scores (1-5): reasoning_quality={rq},
engagement={en}, constructive_stance={cs}.

Return only the feedback text.
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Ask-the-coach prompt (optional follow-up)}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are a supportive debate coach. The participant just argued a round.
Their arguments:
{arguments}

They ask: {question}

Answer in 2-3 sentences: specific, encouraging, actionable.
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Co-player prompt (content step)}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are writing a single debate argument tile.
Topic: "{topic}"
You argue {side_label} the topic.
Responding to: "{target_text}"
Argument angle: {angle}

Rules:
- ONE grammatically complete sentence. Period at the end.
- Hard character limit; write a complete sentence that fits.
- No question marks. No fallacies.
- Argue in good faith: make the strongest FAIR point for your side.
  No cheap shots, no straw-manning, no exaggeration; a reasonable
  sparring partner, not a troll.
- Sound like a thoughtful student, not a textbook.
- Return your argument text only.
\end{Verbatim}

\section{Point-level coaching layer}
\label{sec:pointlevel}

Within a round, the treatment condition also provides point-level feedback on
individual argument tiles. This layer evaluates a single tile in context and
determines whether to surface a reasoning problem and how to express it in plain
language. It prioritizes precision over recall: a missed weak argument is
preferable to a false positive, since spurious feedback interrupts play and erodes
trust in the system.

The first-wave categories, with participant-facing labels in italics, are:
lack of specificity (\emph{Needs more detail}), overgeneralization
(\emph{Too broad}), false causation (\emph{Causal leap}), and irrelevant rebuttal
(\emph{Needs clearer connection}). Rarer categories (exaggeration, anecdotal
evidence, appeal to authority, straw man, whataboutism) are retained for research
analysis only, as they are less reliably detectable from a short tile.

Feedback generation proceeds in stages: evaluate the tile in context, determine
whether a target issue is present, determine whether it is severe enough to
surface, and, if so, produce a brief explanation and an optional rewrite. Any
rewrite must preserve the participant's original stance; a rewrite that alters the
side undermines the participant's intent.

\section{Open parameters}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The exact minimum number of turns per round and the strictness of the
  requirement to reply directly to the co-player (Section~\ref{sec:procedure}).
  \item Final scoring anchors for each of the six dimensions, in particular
  constructive stance, which may exhibit a ceiling if it rewards only the absence
  of hostility.
  \item The composition of the validation coding team (a single expert coder, or
  two coders to establish inter-rater reliability).
  \item The judge model. The source evaluation framework used Gemini 2.5 Pro and
  Claude 3.7 Sonnet; the present study uses Claude Sonnet 4.5.
\end{enumerate}
