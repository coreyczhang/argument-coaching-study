\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This study tests whether tailored AI-generated coaching tips between rounds of debate will help people build stronger arguments over time. Participants play three rounds against an AI partner. We track how their argument quality improves across rounds and compare improvements between players who receive coaching and those who practice on their own. Our setup adapts the practice-and-feedback framework from Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245), who showed that targeted AI feedback builds empathic communication skills. We test whether that same approach works for analytical debate. An AI judge scores each round across six dimensions using the validation setup established by Kumar et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025). This document provides detail about our study design, behavioral measures, evaluation prompts, and preregistered analyses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online arguments deteriorate quickly. Much of that comes down to text itself. When people read an opposing view instead of hearing someone speak it, they rate the writer as less thoughtful and dismiss them more easily (Schroeder, Kardas, \& Epley, 2017). Without voice tone and facial cues, text-based arguments can quickly turn hostile.

Structured debate games offer a practical way to counter this trend. In \emph{Point Taken}, players build, position, and revise their arguments deliberately. A normal comment thread rewards fast replies. This format slows players down and makes them think. In an initial pilot ($N = 38$), this structured format helped participants better understand opposing stances, show more intellectual humility, and stay more open to learning compared to standard open-ended discussions (Elnakouri et al., 2025).

Recent work also shows that deliberate practice paired with AI-driven feedback improves how people communicate. Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245) demonstrated that people build better empathic communication skills when they practice with an AI partner and receive personalized guidance between attempts. We want to know whether that same approach can teach stronger argumentative reasoning. Building a sound argument relies on logic, structure, and evidence more than on emotional connection. Whether AI coaching can cultivate those specific skills is an open question.

\paragraph{Research question.} Does brief, targeted AI coaching between debate rounds improve argument quality more than repeated practice alone? We hypothesize that it will. We expect targeted feedback to help players spot logical fallacies in their own reasoning and build more cogent arguments.

\section{Why now}
\label{sec:whynow}

Two recent developments make this study relevant. First, people are using AI more and more to write, research, and evaluate arguments. Learning to reason alongside these tools has become a valuable skill in its own right. Second, the underlying language models are finally capable of running this kind of experiment reliably. The setup requires an AI opponent that argues fairly in real time along with an automated judge that grades debate quality reliably enough to serve as our primary dependent measure. That level of reliable scoring was established only recently (Kumar et al., \emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025). Running this study with earlier models would have introduced too much noise.

\section{Study design}
\label{sec:design}

We use a between-subjects design with two conditions. Participants enter the study and are randomly assigned to one of two tracks:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Control group.} Players complete three debate rounds without coaching feedback.
  \item \textbf{Treatment group (AICoach).} Players complete the same three debate rounds but receive personalized coaching feedback between each round.
\end{itemize}

Both groups debate the same topics, face the same AI opponent, complete three rounds, and are graded with the same scoring rubric. The only difference is whether the participant sees the coaching interface between rounds. Each participant debates an AI partner. This lets us collect data asynchronously and skip live matchmaking. Each round focuses on a distinct topic and we randomize the topic order for every participant to prevent specific topics from confounding progress over rounds.

Participants remain in their assigned condition across all three rounds. We avoid a within-subjects design because coaching effects would easily bleed into uncoached rounds and that could obscure what a genuine baseline looks like. Keeping participants in separate groups protects the integrity of our control condition.

We prompt the AI opponent to argue strictly in good faith. Our goal is to measure how well someone constructs sound arguments against a fair partner. If the AI used personal attacks or straw men, we would be testing something different. We would be measuring whether participants can handle bad-faith tactics. That is a separate question.

\section{Procedure: rounds and interaction requirements}
\label{sec:procedure}

Each session consists of three rounds on distinct topics in random order and takes about 15 to 20 minutes to complete. To finish a round, a participant must submit at least three argument turns. Additionally, at least one of those turns must directly address a claim the opponent made earlier in the round. This reply requirement guarantees meaningful dialogue, which lets us measure how well the participant engages with opposing points. Section~\ref{sec:open} discusses our current minimum thresholds.

\section{Measures}
\label{sec:measures}

An automated LLM judge scores each completed round across \textbf{six distinct dimensions} on a 1 to 5 scale. Following Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245), we split these into three constructive behaviors and three counterproductive behaviors:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Constructive behaviors (shown to coached participants):} reasoning quality, engagement, and constructive stance.
  \item \textbf{Counterproductive behaviors (tracked for analysis only):} fallacies, evasion, and hostility.
\end{itemize}

We calculate two summary metrics for each round:
\begin{align*}
  \texttt{composite} &= \text{mean of the three constructive dimensions} \quad (1\text{--}5),\\
  \texttt{analysis\_overall} &= \textstyle\sum(\text{constructive}) - \sum(\text{counterproductive}).
\end{align*}
The \texttt{composite} score serves as our primary dependent variable. The \texttt{analysis\_overall} score mirrors the prescriptive-minus-proscriptive index used in our reference paradigm to capture the six-dimension profile.

We show treatment participants only their three constructive scores. Keeping the three counterproductive scores behind the scenes prevents participants from gaming a single composite metric. These negative metrics correspond directly to our point-level feedback rules in Appendix~\ref{sec:pointlevel}. Overgeneralization aligns with fallacies, irrelevant replies align with evasion, and personal attacks align with hostility. This alignment ensures our turn-by-turn guidance and round-level scores evaluate the exact same argumentative habits.

Participants also complete a baseline survey covering their initial topic stances and four dispute-resolution habits followed by a brief exit survey.

\subsection{Rationale for the six dimensions}
\label{sec:whydims}

Our rubric balances what strong reasoning requires with what it must avoid. Every dimension draws from established argumentation research.

Constructive dimensions (shown to treatment participants):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Reasoning quality.} Evaluates whether claims are supported by concrete reasons, examples, or evidence, and whether conclusions follow logically from premises. Strong reasoning relies on verifiable facts; weak reasoning leans on unsupported opinion. This maps directly onto the \emph{cogency} criteria from Wachsmuth et al. (2017), which require claims to be acceptable, relevant, and sufficient. It also reflects the \emph{justification} requirement in the Discourse Quality Index (Steenbergen et al., 2003).
  \item \emph{Engagement.} Evaluates whether the participant tackles the opponent's core points directly and represents them fairly. This addresses the dialectical requirement in Wachsmuth's framework and reflects the critical discussion rules in pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren \& Grootendorst, 2004). Those rules require you to engage the real standpoint. Attacking a weakened version breaks them.
  \item \emph{Constructive stance.} Evaluates whether the participant keeps the focus on the ideas, concedes valid counterarguments, and stays open to nuance. This draws from two key criteria in the Discourse Quality Index: \emph{respect} toward opponents and their ideas, and \emph{constructive politics}, which captures an openness to finding common ground.
\end{itemize}

Counterproductive dimensions (scored internally for analysis, where higher values indicate more frequent flaws):
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{Fallacies.} Captures common informal reasoning errors like overgeneralizations, unwarranted causal leaps, and exaggerated claims (Walton, 1995). We focus specifically on four common flaws observed in our pilot data: vague assertions, overbroad claims, unsubstantiated causal leaps, and non-sequiturs.
  \item \emph{Evasion.} Captures moves that sidestep a central point without offering a real counterargument. This represents a failure of relevance and stands as the direct opposite of engagement.
  \item \emph{Hostility.} Captures bad-faith rhetoric, dismissive personal attacks, and ad hominem insults. This marks the complete breakdown of interpersonal respect and constructive deliberation.
\end{itemize}

Dividing the rubric into three constructive and three counterproductive dimensions captures both productive habits and common pitfalls. This structure allows the coach to guide players toward clear arguments while steering them away from bad-faith shortcuts.

\section{Automated evaluation and validation}
\label{sec:judge}

We score every round with a dedicated call to an LLM judge (\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}); Appendix~\ref{sec:prompts} lists the complete judge prompt. Because our primary conclusions depend directly on these automated ratings, we carefully validate the model's accuracy against human expertise following the methodology in Kumar et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025).

We will have human experts score a benchmark sample of 20 to 30 rounds using the identical rubric. We will measure agreement between human coders and the LLM using Krippendorff's $\alpha$, and we will benchmark model accuracy against inter-rater agreement between two independent human coders. If the LLM underperforms on any dimension during pilot validation, we will refine the prompt instructions and scoring anchors before launching the main experiment. We will also use these validated human-coded examples as few-shot exemplars inside the judge prompt.

\section{Preregistered analysis plan}
\label{sec:analysis}

We are preregistering our hypotheses and analysis scripts before collecting the primary experimental data. An initial test batch ($n = 5$) will run strictly to verify data logging and API latency, and we exclude it entirely from the analysis sample.

\paragraph{Primary hypothesis (H1).} Participants who receive AI coaching will show greater gains in argument quality across the three rounds than participants who practice without coaching. We test this hypothesis using the condition-by-round interaction term in a linear mixed-effects model:
\[
  \texttt{composite} \sim \text{condition} \times \text{round}_c + (1 \mid \text{participant}),
\]
where $\text{round}_c$ is centered at zero ($0, 1, 2$). A positive, statistically significant interaction coefficient will support H1, and we will report the effect alongside its 95\% confidence interval. If the mixed-effects model fails to converge cleanly due to sample size constraints, we will evaluate the difference in change scores from round 1 to round 3 using a Welch $t$-test.

\paragraph{Secondary hypotheses (H2 and H3).} We will fit the same mixed-effects model to the full six-dimension composite (\texttt{analysis\_overall}) and to the summed counterproductive score. This tests whether coaching systematically reduces fallacies, evasive moves, and hostile remarks over time.

\paragraph{Exploratory analyses (H4).} We will track round-over-round trajectories for each individual rubric dimension to identify which specific skills respond most strongly to coaching. We treat these tests as exploratory and report them without corrections for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Exclusion criteria.} We will exclude participants who fail to complete all three rounds, those who trigger effort checks (such as submitting blank text or repetitive single-character strings), and those who report using outside AI assistants like ChatGPT to write their arguments. This matches the exclusion protocol in Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245). We will also remove duplicate Prolific worker IDs and any round where an API failure forced the judge or coach to output fallback error text (tracked via our \texttt{model\_name} log). We will document every exclusion and the specific reason for removal.

\paragraph{Sample size.} We plan to recruit roughly 30 participants per condition (about 60 participants total). Rather than aiming for full statistical power, this study serves as an initial proof of concept to estimate the effect size and its confidence bounds, which will inform a larger follow-up trial.

All analyses will run directly from our export pipeline via \texttt{analysis/e18\_analysis.ipynb}.

\section{Evaluation prompts}
\label{sec:prompts}

The following prompts run verbatim in the production environment.

\subsection{Round evaluation prompt}

Every dimension includes concrete anchors at 1, 3, and 5. The judge must ground every score in direct quotations from the participant's text. The reasoning anchors assess acceptability, relevance, and sufficiency (Wachsmuth et al., 2017). The constructive stance and hostility anchors adapt the respect and openness criteria from the Discourse Quality Index (Steenbergen et al., 2003).

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are an expert annotator scoring how well a participant argued in one
round of a structured debate game. Score each of six behaviors on a 1-5
scale using the anchors below. Judge only the participant's arguments, in
the context of the topic and the opponent's points, and base every score
on specific evidence in their text.

HELPFUL behaviors (constructive argumentation):
1. reasoning_quality — are the claims acceptable, relevant to the point,
   and sufficiently supported (a reason, example, mechanism, or evidence)?
   1 = bare assertions or non-sequiturs; 3 = partial or general support;
   5 = each key claim is specific and backed by a clear reason or example.
2. engagement — does the participant take on the opponent's strongest
   point and represent it fairly, rather than talking past it?
   1 = ignores the opponent; 3 = responds to a weaker version;
   5 = directly answers the opponent's strongest point, stated fairly.
3. constructive_stance — critiques the idea (not the person), grants valid
   points, stays open?
   1 = closed or dismissive; 3 = civil but no openness;
   5 = engages the idea and concedes or builds on a fair point.

COUNTERPRODUCTIVE behaviors (score HIGHER when MORE present):
4. fallacies — overgeneralization, false cause, exaggeration.
   1 = none; 3 = one clear instance; 5 = pervasive.
5. evasion — ignoring points, shifting subject, whataboutism.
   1 = stays on point; 3 = dodges once; 5 = repeatedly avoids.
6. hostility — personal attacks, straw-manning, dismissiveness.
   1 = none; 3 = one loaded remark; 5 = repeated bad-faith attacks.
{examples}
Topic: {topic}
The participant's arguments this round:
{arguments}

Score strictly against the anchors; each rationale must quote or point to
specific text. Respond with ONLY a JSON object with all six keys plus a
"feedback" field (one or two forward-looking tips for the next round).
\end{Verbatim}

The \texttt{\{examples\}} variable holds human-scored few-shot exemplars. Kumar et al. (\emph{Nature Machine Intelligence}, 2025) found that combining a structured rubric with a handful of scored examples substantially improves grading reliability compared to zero-shot prompting. Two provisional examples currently seed this prompt and will be replaced by human-validated pilot rounds prior to data collection.

\subsection{Between-round coaching prompt}

The coaching interface mirrors a one-on-one tutoring session. It highlights a clear strength, focuses on the participant's lowest-scoring constructive skill, and walks through a concrete rewrite of one of their arguments. This adapts the personalized feedback structure from Kumar et al. (arXiv:2603.15245).

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are an argument coach giving one participant feedback between rounds
of a debate game. You have the arguments they just made and their rubric
scores. Help them argue better next round, the way a good writing tutor
would: specific, warm, and focused on one or two changes.

Write feedback in three short parts, addressed to "you":
1. Strength: name one thing they did well, quoting their own words.
2. Focus: take the LOWEST-scoring helpful dimension and explain, in plain
   language, the single change that would move it most. Tie it to a
   specific argument of theirs.
3. Try this: rewrite ONE of their weaker arguments into a stronger version
   that keeps their side and intent, so they see the change concretely.

Under 120 words. Encouraging, no jargon, no numeric scores in the text.

Topic: {topic}
Their arguments: {arguments}
Helpful-dimension scores (1-5): reasoning_quality={rq},
engagement={en}, constructive_stance={cs}.

Return only the feedback text.
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Debate partner prompt}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are writing a single debate argument tile.
Topic: "{topic}"
You argue {side_label} the topic.
Responding to: "{target_text}"
Argument angle: {angle}

Rules:
- ONE grammatically complete sentence. Period at the end.
- Hard character limit; write a complete sentence that fits.
- No question marks. No fallacies.
- Argue in good faith: make the strongest FAIR point for your side.
  No cheap shots, no straw-manning, no exaggeration; a reasonable
  sparring partner, not a troll.
- Sound like a thoughtful student, not a textbook.
- Return your argument text only.
\end{Verbatim}

\section{Turn-level feedback layer}
\label{sec:pointlevel}

Within each round, participants in the treatment condition also receive real-time tips on individual argument tiles. This layer evaluates each tile in conversation context, checks for common reasoning errors, and explains how to fix them using everyday language. We deliberately tune this detector for high precision over high recall. Missing a flawed argument is harmless, but surfacing a false critique breaks the player's momentum and damages their confidence in the coach.

Our primary feedback rules focus on four common flaws: lacking detail (\emph{Needs more detail}), overgeneralizing (\emph{Too broad}), assuming causality without proof (\emph{Causal leap}), and talking past the opponent (\emph{Needs clearer connection}). Less common flaws like anecdotal generalizations, appeals to authority, and whataboutism remain flagged in our backend logs for exploratory research, since short argument tiles rarely contain enough text to detect them reliably.

The feedback engine runs through four steps: it analyzes the tile in context, flags target reasoning errors, checks whether the error is severe enough to justify interrupting the player, and suggests a short explanation with an optional rewrite. Any suggested rewrite keeps the participant's original stance. The tool sharpens the player's own idea. It never puts words in their mouth.

\section{Open parameters}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item The minimum number of turns required per round and how strictly the system verifies direct replies to the debate partner (Section~\ref{sec:procedure}).
  \item Final calibration of rubric anchors across all six dimensions, particularly constructive stance. We want top marks to require building on ideas. Just avoiding insults should not be enough.
  \item The composition of our human validation team (evaluating whether one expert rater is sufficient or if two independent coders are required to benchmark inter-rater reliability).
  \item The final choice of evaluation model. While earlier validation studies tested Gemini 2.5 Pro and Claude 3.7 Sonnet, our current implementation uses Claude Sonnet 4.5.
\end{enumerate}
