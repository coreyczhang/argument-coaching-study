\section{What this document is}

This is the single place where I keep the design, the measures, the prompts, and
the analysis plan for the argument-coaching study, so Aakriti, Steve, and I are
all reading the same thing instead of chasing separate files. The prompts here
are the exact ones running in the live study, not paraphrases. When I change a
decision I update it here and note it in Section~\ref{sec:open}.

Two layers live in this project. The first is the \textbf{experiment}: do people
argue better across rounds when they get an AI coach between rounds. The second
is the \textbf{point-level coaching} that the coach uses (the per-tile fallacy
feedback), which I wrote up first and kept in Section~\ref{sec:pointlevel}. Most
of this document is now about the experiment, since that is what we are running.

\section{Study design}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Type:} between-subjects, two conditions, randomized 50/50 the
  first time a participant loads the study. Between-subjects means each person is
  in one condition only. Nobody does both, and there is no pairing of two humans.
  \item \textbf{Control:} three rounds of the argument game, no coach.
  \item \textbf{AICoach:} the same three rounds plus the between-round coach.
  \item \textbf{Opponent:} each participant argues solo against a live LLM
  co-player, not another participant. That is why we can run it on Prolific with
  single participants and no scheduling.
  \item \textbf{Topics:} one topic per round, topic order randomized per
  participant so topic is not confounded with round.
\end{itemize}

I chose between-subjects so the coach never touches the control group. I chose a
reasonable, good-faith co-player (not an adversarial one) because I want to
measure how well someone argues against a fair partner. An adversarial partner
would be testing whether people resist bad-faith tactics, which is a different
question than the one we are asking.

\section{The game: length and minimum interaction}
\label{sec:length}

This is one of the design choices Aakriti asked me to think through. The goal is
enough interaction per round to (a) give the judge a real sample to score and
(b) give the coach something to work with, without making the study so long that
Prolific participants drop out.

My current plan: a round is defined by \textbf{exchanges}, not just standalone
tiles. Each round requires at least three participant turns, and at least one or
two of those turns has to be a direct response to the co-player's reply rather
than a fresh unrelated point. That guarantees both sides actually engage, which
is what makes the \textit{engagement} dimension measurable. Across three rounds
that is roughly nine to twelve participant messages, which should land around
15--20 minutes. The exact minimums are still an open number I want to settle
(Section~\ref{sec:open}); in the current build the finish gate is three
arguments per round.

\section{What we measure}
\label{sec:rubric}

Each round is scored by an LLM judge on \textbf{six dimensions, 1--5}, following
the RPG paper's preregistered structure (three prescriptive and three
proscriptive dimensions, with the overall score being prescriptive minus
proscriptive). Only the three helpful dimensions are shown to AICoach
participants. I show a subset on purpose: if people saw all six they would
optimize a single number, and the point is to shift how they argue, not to chase
a score.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Helpful (shown):} reasoning quality, engagement, constructive
  stance.
  \item \textbf{Counterproductive (analysis only, never shown):} fallacies,
  evasion, hostility.
\end{itemize}

Derived per round:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{composite} = mean of the three helpful dimensions. This is the
  primary outcome.
  \item \texttt{analysis\_overall} = sum(helpful) $-$ sum(counterproductive),
  the full six-dimension measure.
\end{itemize}

The three counterproductive dimensions line up with the point-level fallacy
categories in Section~\ref{sec:pointlevel}: overgeneralization maps to
fallacies, irrelevant rebuttal maps to evasion, and personal attack / straw man
map to hostility. So the fine-grained tile feedback and the round-level rubric
are measuring the same behaviors at two grain sizes.

\subsection{On gold-standard annotations}

We do not have expert-scored gameplays from a previous pilot, and the judge is
currently zero-shot. So the plan is to build the annotations, not assume them.
After the 5-person pilot I will have Steve (and ideally a second coder so we get
inter-rater reliability) score roughly 20--30 rounds on this same six-dimension
1--5 rubric. Those expert scores get used two ways: as few-shot examples in the
judge prompt, and to compute agreement between the LLM and the human coders
(Krippendorff's alpha), benchmarked against how well the two humans agree with
each other. If a dimension has weak agreement, I revise its anchors before I
trust the confirmatory tests. This happens on pilot data, before the full run.

\section{The LLM prompts (live)}

These are the exact prompts in the running study.

\subsection{Round scoring / judge prompt}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are scoring how well a participant argued in one round of a debate
game, on six behaviors. Score each on a 1-5 scale (1 = not at all
present, 5 = strongly present).

HELPFUL behaviors (constructive argumentation):
1. reasoning_quality — claims are specific and backed by a reason,
   example, or mechanism; conclusions follow from premises.
2. engagement — directly takes on the opponent's strongest points and
   represents their view fairly, rather than talking past them.
3. constructive_stance — critiques the idea not the person; acknowledges
   any valid points; curious and open.

COUNTERPRODUCTIVE behaviors (higher = more of the bad behavior):
4. fallacies — overgeneralizing, false cause, or exaggeration; sweeping
   unsupported claims.
5. evasion — ignoring the opponent's points, changing the subject, or
   whataboutism.
6. hostility — personal attacks, straw-manning, or dismissing the other
   side's view.
{examples}
Topic: {topic}
The participant's arguments this round:
{arguments}

Respond with ONLY a JSON object with all six keys plus feedback.
\end{Verbatim}

The \texttt{\{examples\}} slot is where the expert-coded few-shot examples get
inserted once we have them. Empty means zero-shot, which is the current default.

\subsection{Coach (ask-the-coach) prompt}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are a supportive debate coach. The participant just argued a round.
Their arguments:
{arguments}

They ask: {question}

Answer in 2-3 sentences: specific, encouraging, actionable.
\end{Verbatim}

\subsection{Co-player prompt (content step)}

\begin{Verbatim}[fontsize=\small]
You are writing a single debate argument tile.
Topic: "{topic}"
You argue {side_label} the topic.
Responding to: "{target_text}"
Argument angle: {angle}

Rules:
- ONE grammatically complete sentence. Period at the end.
- Hard character limit; write a complete sentence that fits.
- No question marks. No fallacies.
- Argue in good faith: make the strongest FAIR point for your side.
  No cheap shots, no straw-manning, no exaggeration — a reasonable
  sparring partner, not a troll.
- Sound like a thoughtful student, not a textbook.
- Return your argument text only.
\end{Verbatim}

\section{Point-level coaching (per-tile fallacy feedback)}
\label{sec:pointlevel}

This is the coaching layer the AICoach condition uses inside a round. It looks at
a single argument tile and decides whether to flag a reasoning problem and how to
explain it in plain language. My priority here is precision over recall: missing
a weak argument is better than repeatedly flagging fine ones, because false
positives interrupt play and make people distrust the system.

The first-wave categories I use, with the player-facing label in italics:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lack of specificity (\textit{Needs more detail})
  \item Overgeneralization (\textit{Too broad})
  \item False causation (\textit{Causal leap})
  \item Irrelevant rebuttal (\textit{Needs clearer connection})
\end{itemize}

Rarer categories (exaggeration, anecdotal evidence, appeal to authority, straw
man, whataboutism) stay research-only for now because they are harder to detect
reliably from a short tile and produce noisier classifications.

The prompt separates the task into stages: evaluate the tile in context, decide
whether a target issue is present, decide whether it is strong enough to show,
and only then produce a short explanation and an optional rewrite. Rewrites have
to preserve the participant's original stance; a rewrite that flips or softens
the side undermines trust.

\section{Analysis and preregistration plan}
\label{sec:analysis}

The confirmatory tests are frozen before the full run. The pilot (n=5) is for
checking the pipeline and timing, not for inference.

\paragraph{H1 (primary).} AICoach participants improve across rounds more than
Control. Model: \texttt{composite \textasciitilde{} condition * round\_c +
(1 | participant)}. The condition-by-round interaction is the test; positive
supports H1. If the mixed model will not fit the pilot n, the fallback is a
Welch t-test on each participant's round-1 to round-3 change (difference in
differences).

\paragraph{H2--H3 (secondary).} Same model on \texttt{analysis\_overall} (the
full six-dim measure) and on the summed counterproductive score (does coaching
reduce fallacies / evasion / hostility across rounds).

\paragraph{H4 (exploratory).} Which individual dimensions move most. Descriptive,
not corrected for multiple comparisons.

\paragraph{Rubric validation.} The LLM-vs-human agreement described in
Section~\ref{sec:rubric}, run on pilot data before the full study.

\paragraph{Exclusions (prespecified).} Did not finish all three rounds; failed an
effort check (empty or single-character arguments for a whole round); duplicate
Prolific ID; a scored round that fell back to canned text because the coach or
opponent errored.

The analysis runs from \texttt{analysis/e18\_analysis.ipynb} on the study's data
export.

\section{Open decisions I still want to settle}
\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The exact minimum turns per round and how strictly to require a direct
  reply to the co-player (Section~\ref{sec:length}).
  \item Final anchors for each of the six dimensions, especially constructive
  stance, which I think can hit a ceiling if it only rewards not being rude.
  \item Who codes the validation sample (Steve alone, or Steve plus a second
  coder for inter-rater reliability).
  \item Model choice for the judge. The eval-framework paper used Gemini 2.5 Pro
  and Claude 3.7 Sonnet; we are on Claude Sonnet 4.5.
\end{enumerate}
